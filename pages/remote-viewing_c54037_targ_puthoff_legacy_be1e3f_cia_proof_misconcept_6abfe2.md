## Introduction

The CIA's involvement with [remote viewing]({{ 'remote-viewing/' | relative_url }}) is often presented as if it settled the question of whether psychic perception is real. It did not. What government interest demonstrated was that intelligence agencies considered the possibility worth investigating during the [Cold War]({{ 'cold-war/' | relative_url }}), not that they concluded the phenomenon worked. This distinction is central to understanding the legacy of Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff's research. Their work at Stanford Research Institute (SRI) attracted government funding because intelligence organisations routinely examine unconventional ideas that might offer even a small strategic advantage. The existence of classified programmes and declassified documents therefore reflects official curiosity and risk management rather than official validation.

The misconception persists because many people remember that the CIA funded or inherited parts of the programme, but not how the programme ended. When the intelligence community commissioned an independent review in 1995, the overall judgement was that, despite some intriguing experimental findings, remote viewing had not produced information reliable enough to support intelligence operations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_Project_%28U.S._Army_unit%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2UC Davis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span></span></span>

## What agency interest actually meant

Government agencies investigate many possibilities that ultimately prove unsuccessful. During the Cold War, the cost of overlooking a genuine breakthrough could have been far greater than the cost of funding a relatively modest research programme. Intelligence organisations therefore explored topics ranging from unconventional aircraft concepts to behavioural science, cryptography and alleged psychic phenomena.

The remote-viewing programme evolved through several code names and sponsors over more than two decades, involving the CIA, the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA), Army Intelligence and defence contractors. Funding shifted between organisations as priorities changed. This institutional history is sometimes mistaken for continuous endorsement, when it was largely an ongoing effort to determine whether any operational value existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_Project_%28U.S._Army_unit%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span></span></span>

Importantly, intelligence agencies distinguish between research and operational capability. A project may receive funding precisely because its usefulness is uncertain. The existence of classified contracts therefore says little by itself about whether the underlying hypothesis was confirmed.

## Why Cold War uncertainty encouraged funding

The strategic environment of the 1970s helps explain why remote viewing received serious attention despite extraordinary claims.

Several factors encouraged investigation:

* **Concern about Soviet research.** American intelligence received reports that the Soviet Union was investing in parapsychology and related subjects. Even if those reports were exaggerated, officials worried that ignoring them could create a strategic blind spot.
* **Relatively low research costs.** Compared with major weapons programmes, exploratory studies into [anomalous cognition]({{ 'not-clairvoyance/' | relative_url }}) required modest budgets.
* **High potential payoff.** If remote viewing had proved reliable, even occasionally, it might have transformed intelligence gathering by allowing access to otherwise inaccessible [targets]({{ 'targets/' | relative_url }}).
* **Scientific uncertainty.** Some early laboratory results appeared statistically unusual enough to justify further testing, even though they remained controversial.

This logic was consistent with broader Cold War decision-making. Intelligence agencies frequently funded exploratory research without assuming that every project would succeed. In that context, remote viewing represented a speculative hedge against uncertainty rather than acceptance of paranormal claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/cia-esp-espionage-soviet-union-cold-war" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[History+2Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cia esp espionage soviet union cold war</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The CIA Recruited &#x27;Mind Readers&#x27; to Spy on the Soviets in...17 Oct 2018 — The CIA, Army and Defense Intelligence Agency recruited...</span></span></span>

## Why evaluation did not become endorsement

One reason the CIA connection is misunderstood is that many accounts stop at the existence of the programme rather than its assessment.

In 1995, after the programme had accumulated roughly two decades of work, the CIA commissioned the American Institutes for Research (AIR) to conduct an independent review. Two prominent reviewers reached different conclusions about the scientific evidence.

Statistician Jessica Utts argued that some experimental results showed statistically significant effects that merited further scientific investigation. However, psychologist Ray Hyman agreed that some statistical findings appeared unusual while arguing that methodological weaknesses, insufficient independent replication and alternative explanations prevented concluding that psychic functioning had been demonstrated. Both reviewers therefore recognised that the evidence raised interesting scientific questions, but they differed on how much confidence it deserved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267978941_Evaluation_of_Program_on_%27Anomalous_Mental_Phenomena%27" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2UC Davis]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evaluation of Program on &#x27;Anomalous Mental Phenomena&#x27;PDF &#124; Jessica Utts and I were commissioned to evaluate the research on r...</span></span></span>

The broader AIR assessment focused on intelligence usefulness rather than laboratory [statistics]({{ 'statistics/' | relative_url }}). It concluded that remote-viewing reports were generally too vague, inconsistent and unreliable to support operational decision-making. According to the review, no verified intelligence successes justified continuing the programme as an intelligence collection tool. The CIA subsequently terminated the effort.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_Project_%28U.S._Army_unit%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span></span></span>

This outcome is frequently overlooked. A government evaluation that identifies interesting anomalies is not the same as a government conclusion that [psychic spying]({{ 'psychic-spying/' | relative_url }}) works.

## How public memory turned evaluation into endorsement

Public memory has simplified a much more complicated history.

Several factors contributed to the misconception:

* **Classification created mystery.** Secret programmes naturally encourage speculation. Once documents became public, many readers assumed [secrecy]({{ 'secrecy/' | relative_url }}) implied extraordinary discoveries rather than experimental uncertainty.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Media emphasis on unusual stories.** Dramatic accounts of individual "hits" attract more attention than discussions of inconsistent overall performance or failed sessions.
* **Confusion between agencies.** Many reports refer simply to "the CIA programme", even though responsibility shifted among several military and intelligence organisations over the years.
* **Selective storytelling.** Popular books, documentaries and films often highlight remarkable anecdotes while giving less attention to the final operational assessment.

</div>

## Why this distinction matters for assessing Targ and Puthoff's legacy

For Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff, government sponsorship remains one of the strongest arguments advanced by supporters. It is understandable why the association carries weight: respected defence contractors, classified funding and years of official attention make the research appear more credible than typical paranormal investigations.

However, institutional interest should not be confused with scientific confirmation. Governments routinely investigate uncertain possibilities, particularly where national security is involved. Funding reflects a willingness to test a hypothesis under conditions of uncertainty, not a declaration that the hypothesis has been verified.

The enduring debate surrounding Targ and Puthoff therefore turns less on whether intelligence agencies were interested—which is well documented—and more on whether the evidence generated by that interest met the standards required for scientific acceptance and dependable operational use. On that question, the historical record remains mixed: proponents point to intriguing experimental findings, while the intelligence community's own [final review]({{ 'final-review/' | relative_url }}) concluded that remote viewing had not become a reliable intelligence capability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_Project_%28U.S._Army_unit%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit</span></span></span>
