## Introduction

Cold War intelligence agencies did not fund [remote viewing]({{ 'remote-viewing/' | relative_url }}) because they had concluded that [psychic spying]({{ 'psychic-spying/' | relative_url }}) was real. They funded it because they feared the consequences of being wrong if an adversary discovered an unconventional capability first. During the strategic competition between the United States and the Soviet Union, even ideas that appeared highly improbable could justify limited investigation when the potential military payoff seemed enormous and the financial cost of testing was relatively modest. This logic helps explain why programmes that later became known collectively as Stargate survived for more than two decades despite persistent controversy and mixed results. Official reviews show that investigation did not equal endorsement: agencies repeatedly asked whether remote viewing could provide usable intelligence, and ultimately concluded that it had not demonstrated sufficient operational value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00791R000200180005-5.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AN EVALUATION OF THE REMOTE VIEWING PROGRAMDuring. ## Why Soviet Research Changed the Calculation

The strongest driver behind American interest was uncertainty about Soviet activities rather than confidence in psychic phenomena themselves.

Throughout the 1970s, U.S. intelligence received reports that Soviet researchers were investing time and resources in areas broadly described as [psychotronics]({{ 'psychotronics/' | relative_url }}) and parapsychology. Some reports were exaggerated, while others reflected genuine Soviet research into unusual aspects of human performance. Intelligence officials could not easily determine how serious these efforts were or whether they might produce unexpected military applications. In an environment where strategic surprise was considered a major national-security risk, ignoring the subject entirely seemed imprudent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/science/2003/jun/05/research.science1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian+2History]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The GuardianThe remote viewers &#124; Science4 Jun 2003 — Project Stargate, a 23-year, $20m (£12m) programme of psychic spying, or remote view...</span></span></span>

This reflected a familiar [Cold War]({{ 'cold-war/' | relative_url }}) pattern. American agencies also investigated unconventional aircraft, advanced surveillance concepts and other speculative technologies because uncertainty itself carried strategic costs. Remote viewing became another example of this broader "hedging" strategy: officials preferred spending relatively small sums on research rather than risk overlooking something that an opponent might exploit.

## Low-Cost Testing Versus High-Stakes Ignorance

The attraction of remote viewing lay less in the evidence supporting it than in the extraordinary intelligence advantage it promised if it proved genuine.

If someone could accurately describe distant military installations, locate hostages or identify hidden weapons without entering hostile territory, the implications would have been profound. Such a capability would avoid many of the risks associated with satellites, reconnaissance aircraft or human agents.

From a decision-maker's perspective, the calculation looked asymmetric:

* The research budget represented only a tiny fraction of overall Cold War intelligence spending.
* Even a very small probability of success appeared worth investigating because the possible payoff was exceptionally large.
* Early laboratory findings, although disputed, appeared sufficiently intriguing to justify additional testing rather than immediate dismissal.

## Investigation Was Not the Same as Belief

One of the most common misunderstandings about [Stargate]({{ 'stargate/' | relative_url }}) is that government funding proved intelligence agencies believed psychic spying worked.

The historical record suggests something more cautious. Programme documents show repeated efforts to evaluate, measure and validate claimed abilities under controlled conditions. Intelligence officials sought procedures that might distinguish genuine information from coincidence, educated guessing or unconscious cueing. The existence of testing therefore reflected institutional scepticism as much as institutional curiosity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00789R002800180001-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">STAR GATE PROJECT: AN OVERVIEW(U) This document provides a broad overview of the three main activity areas, (foreign assessment, exter...</span></span></span>

Even when remote viewers participated in operational tasks, their reports were generally treated as supplementary material rather than decisive intelligence. Outputs were often compared with information from conventional collection methods instead of replacing them. This cautious handling indicates that many users regarded remote-viewing reports as exploratory leads rather than established evidence.

## Why the Programme Continued Despite Mixed Results

Several factors helped sustain research beyond its initial experimental phase.

First, intelligence work rarely allows immediate yes-or-no answers. Because claimed successes were difficult to evaluate objectively, advocates argued that promising cases justified further study, while critics pointed to methodological weaknesses and inconsistent performance.

Second, [secrecy]({{ 'secrecy/' | relative_url }}) limited outside scrutiny. Classified programmes naturally evolved more slowly because relatively few independent experts could assess their procedures or replicate findings.

Third, changes in sponsorship allowed the work to survive organisational transitions. Responsibility moved among different agencies and programmes under various code names, reflecting continuing interest in determining whether the reported effects could be made operationally reliable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP96-00789R002800180001-2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">STAR GATE PROJECT: AN OVERVIEW(U) This document provides a broad overview of the three main activity areas, (foreign assessment, exter...</span></span></span>

## What Finally Changed Official Thinking

By the mid-1990s, the strategic environment had shifted. The Cold War had ended, reducing the urgency that had originally justified many speculative intelligence projects.

The decisive question also changed. Instead of asking whether laboratory experiments showed statistically interesting effects, reviewers asked whether remote viewing had produced intelligence that commanders could depend upon in real-world operations.

The independent review commissioned by the CIA concluded that although some experimental findings remained subjects of debate, the programme had not generated intelligence of sufficient quality or consistency to justify continued operational use. Information was generally too vague, inconsistent or difficult to verify to support intelligence decisions. ## The Cold War Lesson

The history of psychic spying illustrates less about belief in paranormal abilities than about how intelligence organisations manage uncertainty. During periods of intense geopolitical competition, agencies may investigate ideas with low prior probability when the potential consequences of overlooking a genuine capability appear exceptionally high.

Stargate therefore represents a Cold War insurance policy rather than proof that officials accepted psychic phenomena.
